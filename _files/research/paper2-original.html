A Survey of Data Agents: Emerging Paradigm or Overstated Hype?

Authors

Yizhang Zhu, Liangwei Wang, Chenyu Yang, Xiaotian Lin, Boyan Li, Wei Zhou, Xinyu Liu, Zhangyang Peng, Tianqi Luo, Yu Li, Chengliang Chai, Chong Chen, Shimin Di, Ju Fan, Ji Sun, Nan Tang, Fugee Tsung, Jiannan Wang, Chenglin Wu, Yanwei Xu, Shaolei Zhang, Yong Zhang, Xuanhe Zhou, Guoliang Li, and Yuyu Luo

Corresponding Authors: Guoliang Li (liguoliang@tsinghua.edu.cn) and Yuyu Luo (yuyuluo@hkust-gz.edu.cn)

Abstract

The survey introduces the first systematic hierarchical taxonomy for data agents, addressing terminological ambiguity that "conflates simple query responders with sophisticated autonomous architectures." Inspired by SAE J3016 automotive automation standards, the framework comprises six autonomy levels (L0-L5) that progressively shift responsibility from human to agent. The review encompasses specialized systems for data management, preparation, and analysis, alongside emerging comprehensive platforms with enhanced autonomy, analyzing critical transitions and identifying technical gaps, particularly the L2-to-L3 progression where agents evolve from procedural execution to independent orchestration.

Autonomy Levels Framework (L0-L5)

L0: No Autonomy – Manual Labor

At this foundational level, "all tasks in data management, preparation, and analysis being entirely human-driven," with complete human responsibility for pipeline orchestration and execution. Data agents remain uninvolved. Traditional approaches require humans to manually formulate SQL queries, write data-cleaning scripts, create visualizations, and interpret results—all labor-intensive processes demanding extensive domain knowledge.

L1: Assistance – Preliminary Responses

L1 agents operate within a "stateless, prompt-response framework," offering preliminary assistance for isolated tasks. These systems exhibit "significant advancements in understanding and reasoning," enabling code snippet generation or advice based on embedded knowledge. The defining characteristic: "humans retain task dominance and responsibility for interacting with the environment, as well as integrating, verifying, and optimizing data agents' outputs." They function as nascent intelligent assistants without environmental perception.

L2: Partial Autonomy – Environmental Perception

The first major evolutionary leap occurs here. L2 agents "gain the ability to perceive and interact with their environment, including data lakes, code interpreters, APIs, and other resources." They can "possess memory, invoke external tools, and adaptively optimize their actions based on environmental feedback." Critically, they remain "within human-orchestrated pipelines," evolving from simple responders to "procedural executors operating within human-orchestrated pipelines, where humans remain responsible for managing the overall workflow."

L3: Conditional Autonomy – Self-Orchestration

This represents "a revolutionary leap where data agents evolve from procedural executors into versatile dominators capable of managing diverse and comprehensive data-related tasks." L3 agents can "autonomously orchestrate tailored data pipelines for a wide range of diverse and comprehensive data-related tasks under supervision." The critical shift: humans transition from orchestrators to "supervisors overseeing the data agents' operation," with the agent assuming "dominant role in data-related tasks."

L4: High Autonomy – Proactive Self-Governance

At L4, agents achieve "a high level of autonomy and reliability, eliminating the need for human supervision and explicit task instructions." They can "proactively identify issues worthy of investigation through continuous monitoring and exploration of data lakes, and selectively orchestrate pipelines to tackle self-discovered problems." Humans become "onlookers," while agents take full initiative.

L5: Full Autonomy – Generative Innovation

The ultimate vision: agents "capable of inventing novel solutions and pioneering new paradigms," extending "beyond applying existing methods." They "advance the state-of-the-art in data management, preparation, and analysis, making any form of human involvement unnecessary." This represents complete autonomy with generative capabilities.

Evolutionary Leaps Between Levels

L0→L1

Introduces intelligent assistance, shifting humans from solo practitioners to users of query-responsive assistants.

L1→L2

Agents gain environmental perception, evolving from stateless responders to procedural executors while remaining within human-designed pipelines.

L2→L3

"The transition from L2 to L3 marks a revolutionary leap where data agents evolve from procedural executors into versatile dominators." This involves "shift in task dominance: while L2 data agents operate within human-designed pipelines for specific subtasks, L3 data agents interpret high-level user intentions to autonomously orchestrate end-to-end pipelines across diverse tasks."

L3→L4

Removes supervision, transitioning "from supervised to unsupervised operation and from reactive to proactive engagement."

L4→L5

Enables generative capabilities, allowing "data agents innovate novel methodologies and pioneer new paradigms."

Pipeline Orchestration Frameworks

L2 Pipeline Characteristics

  • Human-designed workflows executed by agents
  • Task-specific procedures within predefined structures
  • Environmental feedback drives tactical adjustments

L3 Pipeline Orchestration

The transition to L3 emphasizes "data agents interpret high-level user intentions to autonomously orchestrate end-to-end pipelines across diverse tasks." This requires:

  • Selection of appropriate operators from available toolkits
  • Dynamic task decomposition based on problem understanding
  • Cross-stage optimization and adaptation
  • Integration across data management, preparation, and analysis phases

Emerging Orchestration Systems

The survey references systems like "iDataLake" that "encompass data linking, pipeline orchestration, and execution for heterogeneous data analytics over data lakes," illustrating evolving platforms supporting comprehensive orchestration.

Technical Gaps in Orchestration

The survey identifies critical limitations in current systems: "limited pipeline orchestration, reliance on predefined operators, incomplete data lifecycle coverage, and deficiencies in strategic reasoning that hinder the current leap" from L2 to L3.

Human Approval Gates and Oversight Mechanisms

L1-L2 Oversight

Humans maintain "task dominance" with complete responsibility for "integrating, verifying, and optimizing data agents' outputs." Verification remains manual and post-hoc.

L3 Supervision Model

At this level, "humans act as supervisors overseeing the data agents' operation." The framework implies:

  • Pre-execution approval mechanisms (implied but not explicitly detailed in provided content)
  • Monitoring of agent-orchestrated pipelines
  • Intervention authority for anomalous behavior
  • Conditional autonomy allowing human override

L4 Delegation

Humans transition to "onlookers," with "full delegation of responsibility." The survey doesn't detail specific oversight mechanisms for L4, suggesting this remains an open research question.

Accountability Frameworks

The survey emphasizes that "explicit allocation of responsibility between human and machine" is essential, following SAE J3016 precedent. This clarifies liability when failures occur and establishes "lines of accountability at each level."

Development Lifecycle Integration Patterns

Data Management Phase

  • Configuration Tuning (system parameter optimization)
  • Query Optimization (SQL efficiency)
  • System Diagnosis (anomaly detection and resolution)

Data Preparation Phase

  • Data Cleaning (error and inconsistency correction)
  • Data Integration (heterogeneous source reconciliation)
  • Data Discovery (metadata extraction and pattern identification)

Data Analysis Phase

  • Structured Data Analysis (TableQA, NL2SQL, NL2VIS)
  • Unstructured Data Analysis (document and multimodal processing)
  • Report Generation (narrative and visual synthesis)

The survey identifies that current systems show "incomplete data lifecycle coverage," with most agents specializing in single phases rather than integrating across the entire pipeline. This represents a key technical gap hindering progression to higher autonomy levels.

Key Findings and Conclusions

Primary Contributions

  1. Taxonomic Framework: "The first systematic hierarchical taxonomy (L0–L5) for data agents, establishing a clear framework to compare existing data agents, delineate capability boundaries, and clarify accountability"
  2. Structured Review: Comprehensive analysis of existing systems organized by increasing autonomy, mapping state-of-the-art across data lifecycle tasks
  3. Gap Analysis: Identification of critical evolutionary leaps and current limitations, "particularly the ongoing L2-to-L3 transition, where data agents evolve from procedural execution to autonomous orchestration"
  4. Forward-Looking Roadmap: Vision for achieving "proactive, generative data agents" through addressing identified technical gaps

Critical Limitations of Current Systems

L1 Constraints

  • Lack environmental perception and interaction
  • Cannot adapt based on feedback
  • Require manual verification and optimization
  • Restricted to atomic, isolated tasks

L2 Glass Ceiling

  • Remain within human-designed pipelines
  • Cannot independently select or optimize procedures
  • Lack strategic reasoning across diverse tasks
  • Cannot discover problems autonomously

L2-to-L3 Transition Gaps

The survey identifies that "data agents currently face significant challenges advancing from L2 to L3," requiring breakthroughs in:

  • Pipeline orchestration across heterogeneous tasks
  • Strategic reasoning about diverse data problems
  • Autonomous operator selection and composition
  • Integration across complete data lifecycle
  • Robust error handling and self-correction

Vision for Future Development

The survey concludes that realizing higher autonomy levels requires:

  • Long-horizon, holistic perspectives across data lifecycle
  • Autonomous problem discovery mechanisms in data lakes
  • Trade-off analysis across competing objectives
  • Generative capabilities enabling paradigm innovation
  • Robust validation frameworks for unsupervised operation

The roadmap positions data agents as "emerging paradigm" rather than "overstated hype," contingent on addressing identified technical gaps and evolutionary leaps systematically.

Conclusion

This survey establishes data agents as an emerging field requiring clarity through systematic classification. By adapting automotive automation standards to data systems, the framework provides practitioners, researchers, and policymakers with precise terminology for assessing capabilities, assigning accountability, and directing research investments. The identified progression from procedural execution (L2) to autonomous orchestration (L3) represents the critical near-term challenge, while L4-L5 visions of proactive and generative agents outline longer-term research frontiers.
